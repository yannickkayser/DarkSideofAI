Nelson (2020)  ·  Methodological Framework

Computational Grounded Theory
Three-Step Analytical Pipeline

AI data labour platform discourse · B2B vs B2W register analysis · Cross-platform corpus (28 domains, ~4.3M tokens)

Research hypotheses — thread running through all three steps
H1a Labour Visibility Gap Labour terms (worker, job, pay, earn) appear at lower relative frequency in B2B client-facing texts than in B2W worker-facing texts.
H1b Automation Myth B2B texts frame AI outputs as autonomous or machine-produced, suppressing mention of the human labour that generates training data.
H1c Strategic Hypervisibility Where B2B texts do foreground human labour, it appears as a quality signal (HITL, oversight, intervention) rather than as labour conditions.
Step 1 · Epistemic role
What does the corpus actually contain?
Unsupervised discovery. No theoretical categories imposed. The corpus speaks before hypotheses are applied.
Step 2 · Epistemic role
What do those patterns mean?
Human interpretation. Close reading directed by Step 1 outputs. Builds the analytical vocabulary Step 3 will operationalise.
Step 3 · Epistemic role
Do the patterns hold at scale?
Confirmatory testing. Dictionary classifiers and embeddings test whether Step 2 interpretations generalise across the full corpus.
1 Step
Unsupervised Discovery · Exploratory

Pattern Detection through Keyness & Co-occurrence

The corpus is interrogated for what statistically distinguishes client-facing (B2B) from worker-facing (B2W) language. No theoretical categories are imposed. The analyst does not yet test H1a–c.

Corpus28 domains, 6,136 pages
Tokens3.57M client / 778K worker
StatisticLog-likelihood G²
Assoc.PMI (±5 token window)
LevelsCross-platform + within-pair
Methods
Keyness Analysis (G²) Identifies terms statistically overrepresented in one subcorpus relative to the other, controlling for overall frequency. Robust to the 6:1 size asymmetry between client and worker subcorpora. unigrams + bigrams (min freq 3)
Co-occurrence Analysis (PMI) For top key terms, computes collocate profiles within ±5 token window. Reveals the discursive neighbourhood of each term — whether 'human' clusters with quality-oversight vocabulary or with body/motion vocabulary. PMI · min cofreq 10 cross-platform
Two Comparison Levels Cross-platform: all B2B vs all B2W — tests structural register difference. Within-pair: appen.com vs crowdgen.com; toloka.ai vs mindrift.ai — controls for company-level style, isolates audience as the causal variable. cross-platform + appen + toloka
Key Empirical Findings
B2B top terms: annotation (8.47‰), datum (18.97‰), description, dataset, duration, image, video, vision, autonomous, model — a technical data-pipeline register devoid of labour vocabulary.
B2W top terms: apply (9.17‰), remote (9.77‰), project, payment, earn, work, code, pay, job — a labour-market / job-seeking register.
'human' co-occurrence (B2B): hitl, oversight, intervention, involvement, judgement, branch — quality-oversight architecture. Supports H1c.
'human' co-occurrence (B2W): reinforcement, rlhf, brilliance, usefulness, verify — RLHF trainer framing (Mindrift-specific). Human as capability benchmark, not labour agent.
Within-pair Appen: datum (G²=4502), model, human, machine, quality — all client-distinctive. project, earn, payment — worker-distinctive. Strong audience-driven asymmetry.
Outputs → feeds Step 2
①
Ranked Keyness Table Every unigram and bigram with G² score, frequency in each subcorpus, relative frequency. Positive = client-distinctive; negative = worker-distinctive. Directs Step 2 close reading toward top-ranked terms.
②
Co-occurrence Profiles Top PMI collocates per key term, per audience. Reveals whether a term's keyness reflects theoretically expected patterns (e.g. human + oversight) or unexpected contexts requiring interpretive revision in Step 2.
③
Platform-level Term Counts Per-domain frequency breakdowns for all key terms. Flags whether a keyness signal is corpus-wide or driven by one outlier platform. Within-pair comparisons isolate audience as causal variable.
Critical constraint: None of these outputs are findings. They are empirical raw material. A high G² score is an invitation to read closely — not evidence for a hypothesis.
Hypothesis status at end of Step 1 H1a — Labour terms pattern visible but untested H1b — Automation terms distinctive in B2B but mechanism unknown H1c — human+oversight co-occurrence detected, interpretation pending

2 Step
Human Interpretation · Guided Close Reading

Interpretive Analysis & Codebook Construction

The analyst reads the actual pages behind the Step 1 patterns, directed by keyness and co-occurrence results. The question shifts from what is distinctive to why — what are these terms doing rhetorically? This step builds the interpretive vocabulary that Step 3 will operationalise.

InputStep 1 outputs (3 types)
MethodSystematic close reading
Export ATop-8 pages per key term
Export BKWIC concordance lines
Export CWithin-pair page samples
Reading Corpus (04_step2_export.py)
Export A — Top Pages per Term The 8 pages with highest relative frequency for each key term. These pages are most responsible for the keyness signal. Reading reveals the typical discursive context — whether 'autonomous' appears in product capability claims or system architecture documentation. A/client/autonomous.txt, etc.
Export B — KWIC Concordance Every sentence containing each focus term, with ±2 sentence context, labelled by platform and audience. Reveals recurring rhetorical patterns across the full corpus. Read for saturation — patterns stabilise after ~50 lines. B/human_client.txt, etc.
Export C — Within-Pair Samples 20-page samples from each side of each company pair. Same company, different audience. Differences cannot be attributed to corporate style — only to audience register. The strongest test of H1a–c at the level of individual text. C/appen_b2b.txt · appen_b2w.txt
Interpretive Work
For each term, establish: Who is the implied agent? What verbs frame the term? What subjects and objects co-occur? What is the rhetorical function (claim, guarantee, differentiator)?
Term collapsing: Some Step 1 terms will collapse into one phenomenon. oversight + intervention + hitl → single HITL quality-assurance frame. These become one dictionary category in Step 3.
Term splitting: Some terms will split. 'model' = AI model (H1b relevant) AND business model (not relevant). Step 2 distinguishes them; Step 3 implements the distinction via collocate-gated dictionaries.
Unexpected findings: Patterns not predicted by H1a–c but visible in the corpus. Flag and describe. These may become additional hypotheses or qualifying observations in the thesis.
Absence as finding: Keyness misses equally-present terms. Step 2 can notice that neither register dignifies workers — or that compensation language is systematically vague even when pay terms appear.
Output → Codebook → feeds Step 3
①
Named Discourse Patterns Each pattern has: a short name (e.g. 'HITL quality frame'), defining terms, 2–3 representative example sentences, hypothesis mapping (H1a/b/c), and a note on how it differs across audience registers.
②
Refined Term Lists For each pattern, the specific lemmas that reliably index it, after splitting polysemous terms and collapsing synonymous ones. These lists become Step 3's dictionary categories — each category is one testable claim.
③
Interpretive Claims Qualitative statements about rhetorical mechanism: 'human' is used in B2B texts to constitute workers as a quality-assurance infrastructure, not as labour agents. Step 3 tests whether this holds statistically across all 5,226 client pages.
④
Embedding Hypotheses If B2B and B2W use words like 'human' and 'worker' in structurally different semantic neighbourhoods, word embeddings trained on each subcorpus should capture this. Step 2 names the dimensions; Step 3b tests them.
Hypothesis refinement in Step 2 H1a — Labour vocabulary mapped to specific erasure mechanisms H1b — Automation framing distinguished from technical documentation H1c — HITL frame vs irreplaceability frame identified as two sub-mechanisms

3 Step
Confirmatory Testing · Hypothesis-Driven

Dictionary Classification & Semantic Embedding Tests

The interpretive claims from Step 2 are tested at scale across the full corpus. Step 3 is split into two complementary methods: dictionary classifiers test lexical predictions directly; word embeddings test whether the semantic structure differs between registers beyond individual terms.

InputStep 2 codebook
3a methodDictionary indices + χ² / t-test
3b methodword2vec per subcorpus
UnitPage-level scores
RoleConfirmatory · findings
Step 3a — Dictionary Classifiers
Dictionary Construction One dictionary per codebook pattern, each containing the lemmas Step 2 identified as reliably indexing that pattern. Each page receives a score = sum of dictionary hits / page token count (relative frequency index). H1a: labour_visibility_dict H1b: automation_myth_dict H1c: hitl_quality_dict
Statistical Tests For each dictionary index: Mann-Whitney U test comparing B2B vs B2W page scores (non-parametric, appropriate for skewed frequency distributions). Effect size reported as rank-biserial correlation. Multiple comparison correction (Bonferroni) applied. α = 0.05 · effect size r
Within-pair robustness check Same dictionary tests run within appen and toloka pairs. A hypothesis confirmed cross-platform AND within-pair has strong evidence. Cross-platform only = possible confound. Within-pair only = idiosyncratic. Both = structural. appen · toloka · cross-platform
Step 3b — Semantic Embeddings
Subcorpus word2vec Models Separate word2vec models trained on B2B and B2W subcorpora. If the same word (e.g. 'human') has structurally different nearest neighbours across models, the semantic difference is not just lexical — it is encoded in the distributional structure of each register. SGNS · window 5 · dim 100–200
Semantic Axis Projection Following Kozlowski et al. (2019): define a semantic axis (e.g. labour–capital) as the vector difference between pole words. Project 'human', 'worker', 'annotation' onto this axis in B2B vs B2W models. A significant shift confirms the register encodes different relational positioning of these concepts. Kozlowski et al. 2019 method
Confirmatory role Embeddings do not generate new hypotheses here — that was Step 1's role. They confirm that Step 2's interpretations reflect genuine semantic structure, not cherry-picked collocations. A pattern confirmed by both dictionary indices and embedding geometry is robust. 3b confirms 3a · not exploratory
Outputs → Thesis Findings
①
H1a Result Labour visibility index: B2B pages score significantly lower than B2W on combined labour vocabulary. Expected direction: B2B << B2W. Confirmed cross-platform + within-pair = strong evidence for systematic erasure.
②
H1b Result Automation myth index: B2B pages score higher on autonomous/automate/machine vocabulary while suppressing human/worker terms. Tests the asymmetric framing claim — output sounds autonomous; production is hidden.
③
H1c Result HITL quality index: B2B pages score higher on human+oversight collocations. Embedding test: 'human' projects toward quality/control axis in B2B models, toward labour/compensation axis in B2W models.
④
Platform-type breakdown Results disaggregated by platform type (crowd market vs enterprise BPO vs impact sourcing) and HQ region (north vs south). Tests whether the discourse patterns vary across the structural diversity of AI data labour markets.
Hypothesis resolution in Step 3 H1a — Confirmed / disconfirmed / qualified by dictionary index test H1b — Confirmed / disconfirmed / qualified by automation index test H1c — Confirmed at lexical level (3a) + semantic level (3b)
